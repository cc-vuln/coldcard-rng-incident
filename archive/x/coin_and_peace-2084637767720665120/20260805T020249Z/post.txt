How Korean Bitcoiners Survived Coldcard—and Issues It Revealed for the Rest of Us



The Coldcard incident which unfolded last weekend sent a massive shockwave through the entire Bitcoin industry, and the dust still hasn't fully settled.


While I was fortunate enough not to be directly affected, this has been affecting me emotionally as well, considering the damage it has caused and the negative future implications for Bitcoin.
Amidst all this, I heard something fascinating from the Korean Bitcoin community regarding their response to the Coldcard incident that I think is worth sharing.  (credit goes to @atomicBTC  &  @K_Bitcoiner)
Simply put, despite there being many Coldcard users in South Korea, there hasn't been a single reported case of direct Bitcoin loss there. They've managed to handle the situation remarkably well.
In contrast, this incident was characterized by the fact that many of the victims were in the English-speaking community—people who actually had relatively high literacy regarding Bitcoin self-custody.
So why was the Korean Bitcoin community able to navigate this situation safely?
I believe this isn't just an issue of technical proficiency or understanding of Bitcoin. It points to a problem that has been worsening in the Bitcoin space for a while now: a lack of neutrality and over-reliance on influencers.


Using this successful Korean case as an example, I want to point out some of the structural issues I have been observing in the English-speaking Bitcoin community in particular.


The Korean Bitcoin Community's "Obsession" with Air-Gapped Self-Custody


As those in the know are aware, the Korean Bitcoin community has an extraordinary passion for self-custody and hardware wallets, especially air-gapped features.


Because of this, Coldcard—which was one of the first to offer air-gapped features—was highly popular among Korean Bitcoin power users. Up until the incident, there was apparently a substantial user base there.


Hearing this, you might assume that many Korean users suffered direct losses in this Coldcard incident. But the reality is that almost no users in Korea were affected according to the local insiders.
Why?
It turns out that Korean community leaders and influencers have been strongly advocating for the "right" way to do self-custody. 


In the context of this incident, they advised users not to trust the random number generation of any specific vendor's wallet, but instead to generate passphrases more securely by rolling physical dice themselves. And crucially, it seems many users actually followed that advice.
This is a deeply impressive case. It's extremely rare to find a community with such a high level of understanding and actionable practice regarding self-custody.


Blind Spots and Bias: How Influencers Amplified the Risk
On the flip side, why did so much damage occur in the English-speaking community, which is generally supposed to be much more information-rich?
As some others have pointed out, I also feel that one of the reasons the damage spread so widely is that some English-speaking influencers and podcasters trusted Coldcard a bit too much.

Don't get me wrong: I don't doubt that the vast majority of influencers act in good faith and try to share the most accurate information possible. In that sense, it would be unfair to put the blame for lost funds entirely on the influencers who recommended Coldcard.
At the same time, whether they were conscious of it or not, quite a few trusted influencers and podcasters in the English-speaking community were sponsored by Coldcard or had personal relationships with the team. Furthermore, their alignment with the Coinkite team's "Bitcoin-only" philosophy likely skewed their objective evaluation and judgment of the product.
As a result, influential community leaders in the English-speaking world placed excessive trust in Coldcard's security. Their followers and other Bitcoiners followed suit, further amplifying inaccurate information and perceptions.


In contrast, Korean influencers—who lacked those direct, close ties and viewed Coldcard from a more neutral perspective—were able to calmly analyze the risks of trusting a specific vendor's product. They recommended that their followers minimize their trust in Coldcard by using methods like dice rolls for random number generation. In the end, this approach protected many users from losing their money.


 "Don't Trust, Verify": A Survival Guide Beyond Code

It all sounds simple in hindsight, but I've recognized for a while now that this over-trust in influencers—and the resulting "echo chamber" of the Bitcoin community based on personal or financial relationships—is a major structural problem.


Especially over the last few years, I can't help but notice a significant deterioration in the overall quality of information and discourse regarding Bitcoin in the English-speaking world, majorly due to this skewed incentive and personal bias. And I believe the Coldcard incident exposed this issue in the worst possible way.


Finally, Bitcoiners' favorite motto "Do not trust, verify" is often used in the context of software development. But I believe this motto should also be applied to how we filter general information outside of software.
In fact, I want to emphasize that simply factoring in the financial and relational biases of the messenger—using basic common sense—can proactively prevent a lot of trouble and accidents.
This is something even users without technical expertise can do. Personally, I believe a big reason I've managed to survive in the wild-west world of Bitcoin for over a decade is that I've been highly sensitive to human biases and incentives.
Taking the successful case of the Korean Bitcoin community and the comparison with the English-speaking community as a lesson, I hope that the community will really reflect on itself and strive to avoid the loss of funds and other potential damages in the future.

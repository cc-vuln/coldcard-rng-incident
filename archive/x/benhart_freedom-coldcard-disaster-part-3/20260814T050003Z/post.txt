COLD CARD DISASTER, Part 3
THE MANY PROBLEMS WITH BITCOIN SELF-CUSTODY

As I mentioned earlier, I was completely sold on Bitcoin self-custody. 

I owned two ColdCard MK4s and a Q.  And had almost all my Bitcoin under self-custody, a small amount on Coinbase.
 
I did create my wallets by rolling dice, and adding a pass phrase. I guess I created my wallets the right way, so I’m told my funds were safe, though I don’t really know.
 
Nevertheless, I instantly wisked my funds back to Coinbase when news broke of the ColdCard hack — approximately $130 MILLION stolen due to a programming error in the Random Number Generator. 
 
ColdCard was proclaimed the “gold standard” for secure self-custody by the top Bitcoin influencers. 
 
I believed them. 
 
Then I learned that CoinKite (the maker of ColdCard wallets) only has about 5 employees.
 
It’s barely a company.
 
Then I also learned that CoinKite was paying Bitcoin influencers to promote their wallets.
 
Some are suggesting the flaw in the ColdCard Random Number Generator was an inside job.
 
Possible. 
 
But I think it was just incompetence. 
 
This was a five-man shop.  So, obviously, and on hindsight, something like this was bound to happen. 

Hindsight is always 20/20.
 
This is the big problem with self-custody right now. 
 
The tech just isn’t there.
 
Apple, Microsoft, Google, and the Big Tech companies have tens of thousands of developers who work full-time on security.
 
If Apple were to make a self-custody Bitcoin wallet and advertise it as such, I would be more inclined to trust it — even though, I don’t trust the Big Tech companies at all. 
 
But I trust them a lot more than I do the shoestring Bitcoin and crypto self-custody wallet operations.

==========
SOFTWARE WALLETS ARE ANOTHER ACHILLES HEEL
==========
 
Another yuge vulnerability for Bitcoin use is the software wallets that are required for interacting with cold storage hardware wallets.
 
For the ColdCard, for example, you also need to download a software hot wallet such as Electrum, Sparrow, or BlueWallet.
 
I was using Electrum, which is the one used by many Bitcoin OGs. Electrum is widely recommended by the Bitcoin influencers.
 
But Electrum is run by one person, its founder Thomas Voegtlin — a Bitcoin OG who launched Electrum in 2011.
 
Motivated by his passion for Bitcoin, he works on Electrum as a volunteer, or mostly volunteer. 
 
Awesome. 
 
Evidently, he can afford to do so because he had accumulated enough Bitcoin in the early days when one Bitcoin cost $2. 
 
Good for him.
 
He has a few other developers involved with Electrum who go under the names ghost43, f321x, and accumulator. 
 
And there are other volunteer developers mentioned on Github who have contributed fixes and patches to Electrum over the years.
 
All these people work essentially as volunteers. 
 
Electrum charges no fees. It runs on donations and tips.
 
I admire Thomas Voegtlin, his passion for Bitcoin, and for freedom.
 
But it’s probably unwise to trust a small group-of part-time volunteers with any substantial amount of your money, much less your life savings.
 
And, indeed, Electrum has been hacked.
 
A famous one was the Sybil FAKE UPDATE and FAKE SERVER exploit of 2018-19 that drained wallets of about 1,980 Bitcoins.  Value today: about $130,000,000 — so similar in scale to the ColdCard hack this past week.
 
This involved attackers setting up thousands of malicious servers. 
 
When an Electrum user attempted a transaction, the malicious server blocked it and returned an official-looking pop-up that said “Your version of Electrum is outdated. Please update to continue.” 
 
You would then be routed to a fake download and your funds stolen.
 
I continue to see pop-up notices like this on Electrum. 
 
I don’t know if these notices that I continue to see on Electrum are legit or not.
 
When one pops up, I always uninstall my Electrum software, go back to the official site, and install the most recent version.
 
I also run a malware check on my computer.
 
But, as we found out with ColdCard, the danger can also be hidden the update from the official site — in the form of coding error, as happened in the ColdCard case.
 
Then there are the Google ads that promote fake versions of Electrum. Same principle. 
 
Victims are taken to a malicious download of fake Electrum software on a site that looks like the official Electrum site.
 
Then there are the clipboard hijackers, the fake support hijackers, and the list goes on.
 
And because Electrum is a small team of essentially volunteer developers existing on donations and tips, they have no way to police any of this the way Apple, Microsoft, Google and Big Tech do.
 
==================
THE SPARROW WALLET
==================
 
Sparrow is another wallet promoted by the Bitcoin OGs.
 
But this is an even smaller outfit than Electrum. 
 
Sparrow Wallet is run by one guy who goes by the pseudonym Craig Raw.
 
 This Craig Raw also fellow exists on donations and tips.
 
Hundreds of millions, if not billions, of dollars are being guarded by this pseudonymous Craig Raw fellow — who might well be a great guy.
 
But I don’t think I’ll trust him with a substantial amount of my money.

===========
BLUE WALLET
===========
 
Another popular software wallet with the Bitcoin OG’s is BlueWallet.

This is also a shoestring outfit — founded in 2017 and run by three guys named Nuno Coelho, Igor Korsakov, and Marcos Rodriguez. 
 
 BlueWallet also charges no fees and relies on donations to exist.
 
And there are other wallets, including Wasabi, Nunchuck, and others — all with the same problem. 

They are run by small teams of developers and exist on donations and tips.

================
NO GETTING AROUND INTERACTING WITH THE INTERNET
================
 
The big problem with self-custody is you still have to interact with the Internet. 

Hardware wallets (like ColdCard, Trezor, Ledger, etc) are constantly updating their firmware, which must be downloaded.
 
Ledger is the biggest company in the self-custody hardware wallet space, with about 900 employees.
 
So at least Ledger is a real company, so is probably better than CoinKite or these other shoestring outfits.
 
But then these hardware wallets, to be used, must interact with software wallets (like Electrum, Sparrow, or BlueWallet) which sit on your computer . . . and must also be constantly updated with new downloads.
 
Even the multi-trillion-dollar Big Tech companies have trouble keeping up with a world full of hackers, now powered by AI — including bad actor regimes like North Korea, Iran, China, etc.
 
So I think self-custody is pretty much dead until the tech gets a lot better.

===============
SELF-CUSTODY WON’T WORK FOR 99% OF PEOPLE
===============
 
Widespread adoption of Bitcoin is required for Bitcoin’s price to keep going up.

For widespread adoption to occur, the user experience needs to get a lot better. 
 
99% of people are not going to . . .
** roll dice 100 times, 
** write down the result of each roll,
** enter the result in the device,
** create a pass phrase with dice roll, 
** set up multi-sig across multiple hardware wallet vendors, to create a self-custody wallet.

Then trust these devices and software that are created by these tiny outfits and informal groups of volunteers.
 
It takes at least 200 hours of study to set up your self-custody wallet the right way. You literally have to become your own IT department.
 
And then, the hackers (with AI tools) still might get you, and probably will.
 
Approximately, 4,000,000 Bitcoins have been lost over the years — value about $260 BILLION. Lost forever, mostly due to user error.
 
The biggest threat to your self-custodied Bitcoin is YOU.
 
If you forget or lose your seed words or pass phrase, your Bitcoin is lost forever. Or if you fail to use proper security protocols, your private key can be stolen by a hacker.

Or if you are storing your Bitcoin on an unregulated exchange (such as Mt Gox in the old days or FTX more recently), your Bitcoin can be stolen, and probably will be.
 
When your Bitcoin under self-custody is lost, there’s no customer service you can call, no insurance policy that will backstop your self-custody set-up. And good luck filling out a police report.
 
Now, I’m still very much a supporter of Bitcoin.  I very much hope it succeeds.
 
The underlying technology of Bitcoin has never been hacked. There are more possibilities for private key codes than there are atoms in the known universe. 
 
The problem is the tech needed to access Bitcoin and transact with it — at least at the retail consumer level.

The tech currently in use for Bitcoin self-custody is no where near ready for prime time.
 
Without knowing the details, I suspect Fidelity has a better, more secure way of interacting with the Bitcoin network than the lone retail consumer. 
 
Fidelity spent a decade or more setting up its Bitcoin custody operation before offering it to customers.
 
When Fidelity Custody manages Bitcoin, they operate with institutional insurance, SOC 2 compliance, multi-signatory key ceremony rooms, and corporate balance sheet backing. 
 
If Fidelity suffers an internal infrastructure failure, they have legal liability, capital, and insurance to make customers whole.
 
I’m glad I now know how to self-custody my Bitcoin — in case there’s some kind of emergency and I need to move my assets to self-custody.
 
So I I’ll keep a bit of Bitcoin in self-custody for fun, and as an escape hatch from the government’s fiat money system.
 
But I am moving 95% of my Bitcoin to Fidelity, which does allow me to wisk my funds into a self-custody wallet if I need to for some reason.
 
I doubt I’ll need to.

For most newbies thinking of wading into Bitcoin, I suggest just buying a little through a Bitcoin ETC, like IBIT.

THE QUANTUM COMPUTING THREAT
=================

The truth is Bitcoin is still a highly speculative asset.
 
We don’t know how Bitcoin will stand up to Quantum computing.
 
There are relatively simple defenses that can be built to Quantum computing. 
 
But Bitcoin’s highly decentralized structure makes it difficult for Bitcoin to respond quickly to threats — requires a 90% consensus among miners and validating nodes to approve changes.
 
Bitcoin’s highly decentralized structure is both Bitcoin’s strength and weakness.

Bitcoin is unchangeable money.  There will never be more than 21 Million Bitcoins. It’s a digital fortress — a digital Fort Knox.

This is Bitcoin’s strength.
 
Troy also looked pretty strong — lasted 200 years or so, according to Homer’s story. But it was eventually defeated by a trick — the Trojan Horse.
 
So we don’t know how Bitcoin will stand up under relentless attack by AI in tandem with quantum computing, which will be here in a few years, probably by the early 2030s.
 
One big worry with Bitcoin is how few developers are even involved with Bitcoin.
 
Bitcoin currently has about six key developers. 
 
This should alarm you. 
 
Michael Saylor, Jack Dorsey, and some others are now paying developers to tend to Bitcoin and trying to get more developers involved.
 
Perhaps as Blackrock, Fidelity and the big financial institutions become increasingly invested in the success of Bitcoin, they will pay developers to be involved.

The notion that Bitcoin would just grow and flourish on its own without Wall Street’s active involvement and participation was a pipe dream.
 
Won’t happen.

Civilization is required.

This all sounds very cool.
 
But, in the real world, there’s no such thing as a “Sovereign Individual.”
 
The “Sovereign Individual” gets eaten by the tribe.

There’s movie I really like called “INTO THE WILD” — about an idealistic young lefty (Emile Hirsch) who decided to exit civilization and live in Alaska on his own, as a “Sovereign Individual.”

There is no such thing as a viable “trustless” society or “trustless money.”
 
If you decide to self-custody because you don’t trust J.P. Morgan, Apple, Google, and Microsoft, you then have to trust something like the ColdCard in combo with the Sparrow Wallet, run by some pseudonymous fellow who goes by the name Craig Raw.
 
I don’t trust J.P. Morgan, Apple, Google, or Microsoft either. Nor do I trust our government.

=================
THE “OPEN SOURCE” CODE MYTH
=================
 
“Open Source” code is another myth we keep hearing about in the Bitcoin community — supposedly the magic bullet that keeps us safe.
 
The theory being that if code is “Open Source,” everyone can look at it and spot bugs.

This is like expecting people to read the “Terms of Service” of every app you use, every website you visit. 
 
“Open Source” code is fine, transparency good.

But even if you are a coding superman, this doesn’t mean you will be able to spot possible bugs in millions of lines of “open source” code.
 
The bug in the ColdCard code sat there as “Open Source” for 5 years in plain view, and no one caught it.
 
It was probably spotted with the help of AI.
 
Turns out the macro flag (MICROPY_HW_ENABLE_RNG) was inadvertently set to zero during the software build.
 
So $130 MILLION stolen.

Live in a way that you can save 10% of your income.

This might mean living with your parents or renting one room to live in for a while, until you are earning more and can afford a better place. 
(You can only live in one room at time anyway)
 
Then take the 10% of your income that you are saving and split up your investments as follows:
 
40% in the QQQ ETF (Nasdaq 100 Index)
40% SPY ETF (S&P 500 Index).
5-10% Bitcoin
 
Low-cost Index funds out-perform professional money managers 80% of the time.
 
If you can find a way to save $300 per month, and if your money compounds at a rate of 12% per year on average, you will have $300,000 in 20 years . . . or $1,067,000 in 30 years.
 
Then if Bitcoin explodes (which is possible), you might do better than this. That’s a potential bonus. It’s like buying a Call Option.
 
When I hear that people lost their life savings because they lost all their Bitcoin in the ColdCard hack, I shake my head and wonder: “What were they thinking?”
 
Never put all your eggs in one basket.
 
One of the most important principles of intelligent investing is intelligent DIVERSIFICATION.
 
Warren Buffett, the greatest investor of all time, practiced extensive diversification. He also made some terrible investments.
 
Buffett says his worst investment was buying the Berkshire-Hathaway mill in 1962. 
 
He says this investment probably cost $200 BILLION in lost compounding returns over time — money that could have been deployed more productively. 
 
He says he allowed emotional attachment to cloud his judgement on this.
 
Ironically, he kept the Berkshire-Hathaway name for his company (after this worst ever investment), probably as a reminder of a hard lesson learned.
 
Other horrendous Buffett investments include Dexter Shoes, Precision Cast Parts, U.S. Airlines.
 
He would not have made these bad investments if he had not been 100% convinced at the time that these were the best possible investments among all options he could find.
 
He also completely missed the Tech and Internet revolution of the 1990s and 2000s. Didn’t invest in Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and only started investing in Apple a few years ago.

Even though Warren Buffet is now worth about $130 BILLION, he still lives in the same house he bought in 1958 for $31,500.

Never treat any investment like a religion. 
 
Never put all your money on one stock or in one asset, no matter how convinced you are about the greatness of an investment.

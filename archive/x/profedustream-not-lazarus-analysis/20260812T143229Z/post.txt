 The hacker who mixed stolen bitcoins yesterday is most likely not the Lazarus Group!

Analysis 

To start with, one on-chain element strikes me as particularly revealing:

 The thief initially had about 65 BTC, stolen on Sunday, August 2 at 6 a.m. from 465 transactions.

Yet, instead of breaking this sum into dozens of small UTXOs before gradually sending them into the Wasabi coinjoin, he did something much more direct:

 He injected the 65 BTC all at once into this coinjoin.

Then he did it again.

At the output of the first Wasabi round, he still held a UTXO of about 54 BTC. And once again:

 He directly reused this huge UTXO as input for a new coinjoin.

And so on.
In other words, this attacker isn't really trying to hide the size of his position.
He moves a massive amount of bitcoins from one round to the next, with relatively simple and repetitive behavior, probably even automated.

Yet, this is generally not how Lazarus operates 

In fact, the pattern usually associated with Lazarus looks more like this:

 Prior fragmentation

The funds are split into hundreds, or even thousands, of smaller UTXOs.
Often amounts in the range of 1 to 2 BTC.

The goal is twofold:
1) multiply the branches to analyze for analysts, and make them lose time;
2) avoid a single UTXO compromising too large a portion of the funds.

 Progressive injection into mixing tools

The Lazarus Group typically doesn't take a batch of several tens of BTC and shove it brutally into a coinjoin.

Their pre-fragmented funds are indeed injected progressively:
- in small batches;
- over multiple rounds;
- sometimes over several days or weeks;
- with periods of dormancy.

 Remixing and dispersion

Their UTXOs then pass through several coinjoins to increase their "anonymity set" and complicate tracking.
It's only after this phase that they are reconsolidated, bridged to other chains (notably the THOR chain, which often comes up in hacks), then converted to stablecoins; before finally being directed to OTC networks.

The classic Lazarus Group pattern is therefore more like:
 Fragment
 Mix progressively
 Remix
 Reconsolidate
 Bridge
 Liquidate

Yet, here, we observe almost the opposite:
 a huge UTXO enters Wasabi;
 a huge UTXO comes out;
 then this same huge UTXO heads back into a new CoinJoin to be fragmented bit by bit.

This doesn't mean Wasabi is ineffective, nor that the perpetrator isn't trying to cover his tracks 

But from a behavioral standpoint, this strategy seems much cruder and far less thoughtful / compartmentalized than the operations typically attributed to Lazarus.

In other words: the hacker we're talking about doesn't seem as skilled as the Lazarus Group.

 Of course, an on-chain pattern never constitutes absolute proof of identity: a group can indeed change methods, test a new strategy, or deliberately imitate another actor.

But here, the gap is significant.

 Because sending 65 BTC, then 54 BTC, in bulk into successive CoinJoins is hard to reconcile with the fragmentation logic, risk dilution, privacy optimization, and spreading observed in Lazarus hacks.

My conclusion:
 This flow is linked to the ColdCard vulnerability, but its operator does not, at this stage, exhibit the typical operational pattern of the Lazarus Group 

The question now remains open regarding wave #1 (1157 BTC), which hasn't moved for several days!
